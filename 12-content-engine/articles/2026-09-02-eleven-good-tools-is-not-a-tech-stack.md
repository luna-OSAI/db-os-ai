## The problem

- Nobody sits down and designs a small business tech stack. It accumulates one sensible decision at a time, and each decision is judged on its own merits rather than on what it does to everything already installed.
- A single connected platform removes the seams between those tools, so one customer record is read by every function instead of the same person existing five times in five systems that disagree with each other.
- What you get back is the quiet stuff you are currently paying for and not seeing: enquiries that stop falling through gaps, follow-up that keeps running when the person who built it is on leave, and a software bill you can state without opening your bank statement.

---

## TL;DR

*Seven things, if you read nothing else*

- **The stack was never chosen, it was accumulated.** Each tool solved a real problem on the day it was bought. What nobody priced was the maintenance cost of the connections between them, because that cost arrives later and never appears on an invoice.
- **The evidence says stacks grow back.** BetterCloud's *2026 State of SaaS* report has the average app count rising again, up 11 per cent year on year, and states that the consolidation era is over. Zylo's *2026 SaaS Management Index* puts unused licences at 36 per cent. Both describe companies at large, not businesses with one to ten staff, so take the mechanism from them rather than the number.
- **The data gap is real and worth naming.** There is no reliable published figure for what an Australian service business with fewer than ten staff actually spends on software each month. The $500 to $1,000 range you will see quoted, including by OS A.I, is an industry estimate rather than a measurement. Treat any vendor number in this space, including ours, as a starting hypothesis you test against your own bank statement.
- **Compliance follows the data, not the tool.** Under the Spam Act 2003, marketing email and SMS need consent, accurate sender identification and a working unsubscribe in every message. When consent lives in one tool and sending happens in another, the record and the send can drift apart. Outbound calling carries obligations under the Do Not Call Register Act 2006. Consolidating does not create these duties, it just makes them easier to evidence.
- **What OS A.I does:** puts CRM, email, SMS, funnels, websites, calendars, payments, courses and community on one platform, with one contact record, a unified inbox across your channels, native workflow automation instead of a connector layer, and an AI troubleshooter that flags broken workflow steps and explains a workflow in plain English.
- **What it does not do:** it does not make a weak offer sell, it does not fix a business with no lead flow, and it does not configure itself. An unconfigured platform is worse than the tools it replaced, because you are now paying for both.
- **Watch the half-migration.** The most expensive version of this project is the one where you buy the platform, move three of your eleven tools across, keep the other eight running as a safety net, and quietly maintain two stacks instead of one.

## It starts with a renewal email

A subscription renewal lands on a Tuesday morning. Ninety-four dollars, a tool you signed up for in a hurry about eighteen months ago to fix a scheduling problem that was, at the time, genuinely doing your head in.

You go looking for it in your bank statement to check what else is coming out. That is when it gets interesting. There is the scheduling tool. There is the email platform. There is a funnel builder you used for one campaign. There is a connector service on an annual plan. There is a form tool, a survey tool that came free with the form tool and then did not, a chat widget, a phone system, a proposal tool, and one line item at $49 a month that you genuinely cannot identify.

Eleven subscriptions. Four of which nobody in the business can fully account for. None of it was a mistake at the time.

That last sentence is the part worth sitting with, because it is what makes this problem so hard to see from the inside. Every tool in that list was bought to solve something real, and mostly did solve it. The stack is not the result of bad decisions. It is the result of eleven good ones, made in isolation, over three or four years.

### CARD: The five-minute stack audit

Do this before you read the rest. Open your bank statement, filter the last full month, and write down four columns.

1. **Tool.** What it is called.
2. **Monthly cost.** The real figure, including annual plans divided by twelve.
3. **Who touches it.** The one person who knows how it is configured.
4. **What it duplicates.** The other tool on this list that already stores the same contact.

> Column four is the one that hurts. Column three is the one that costs you when someone resigns.

---

## The bill is the cheap part

Most owners who run that audit stop at the total. It is a satisfying number to be annoyed about, and it is the wrong number.

The subscriptions are the visible cost. The real cost sits in the seams, in every place where data has to move from one vendor's system to another vendor's system. A seam is where a contact gets created twice with two spellings of the same surname. A seam is where the enquiry from your website form lands in the email tool but never in the CRM, so the follow-up sequence runs and the sales pipeline never knows the person exists. A seam is where you personally re-key something at 9pm because the automation that was supposed to do it stopped working in March.

None of that is billed to anyone. It shows up as a month that was quieter than it should have been, and the explanation you reach for is that you need more leads.

Here is the structural bit. Every tool you add to fix a stack problem adds more seams than it removes, because the new tool has to connect to everything already there. Eleven tools do not have eleven connections between them. They have dozens of possible ones, and you are maintaining whichever subset you happen to have wired up. **The unit of the problem is the stack, not any single tool in it.**

## What the numbers actually say, and what they do not

The research on this changed direction recently, and the honest version of it is more useful to you than the tidy one.

For two years the story was consolidation. Organisations that could measure the cost of sprawl were cutting tools, and average app counts fell. That has reversed. BetterCloud's *2026 State of SaaS* report has the average number of applications per organisation rising again, up 11 per cent year on year, with mid-market companies going from 116 to 164 apps in a single year. The report puts it plainly: the consolidation era is over.

The cause it names is AI. Organisations now run an average of 27 AI-powered SaaS applications, around 22 per cent of the portfolio, and only 56 per cent of the apps in use carry IT approval. Close to half the software inside those companies was bought by somebody who was never asked to think about the stack.

That should sound familiar, because it is your problem at a larger scale. Nobody approved your eleven either. Each one arrived to solve something.

The caveat: those figures describe companies at large, including enterprises with IT departments. An Australian business with four staff is not running 164 apps, and anyone who tells you otherwise is stretching a statistic past where it goes. What travels from that research to your business is the mechanism, not the number. **Stacks grow by default. They shrink only when somebody decides to shrink them, and they grow back the moment that attention moves on.**

The second number is the one you can check yourself. Zylo's *2026 SaaS Management Index*, published 29 January 2026 and built on more than 40 million licences and $75 billion in software spend under management, puts unused licences at 36 per cent when measured against recommended utilisation levels.

Go back to your audit. Count the tools nobody has opened in ninety days, then count the seats you pay for on the tools you do use. If about a third of it is dead, you are ordinary, and you are also paying for it every month.

What nobody has published is a credible figure for Australian small business software spend specifically. It does not exist. OS A.I's own working estimate is a stack of eight to twelve tools costing $500 to $1,000 a month, and that is the company's reasoning rather than a measurement. We flag it as an estimate every time we use it, and you should treat it as one.

## A connector is not an integration

This is where the argument gets precise, and precision matters because the cheap version of it is wrong.

Zapier is excellent. Make.com is excellent. If you have built something serious with either of them, you already know more about your own processes than most owners do. The argument is not that connectors are bad products. It is that **a connector is infrastructure, and you are now maintaining infrastructure you never planned to maintain.**

Every connector is a dependency on two vendors, both of whom will change their API when it suits them and neither of whom will ring you first. Someone renames a field in March. The zap that fed your enquiry form into your CRM starts failing silently that afternoon.

Silently is the whole problem. A broken automation does not send an alert. It does not appear in a dashboard. It just stops, and you find out six weeks later from a customer who says they filled in your form and never heard back.

## The bit nobody prices: the person who wired it up

There is usually one person who knows how it all fits together. Sometimes that is you. Often it is a contractor who set it up two years ago, or the operations person who has quietly become the only one who understands why the ninth step in the workflow exists.

The tell is a sentence: "don't touch that, it'll break."

That is not a technology risk, it is a key-person risk, and almost nobody has priced it. When that person is on annual leave, nothing can be changed. When they leave for good, you inherit a machine with no documentation, no map, and no safe way to test whether a change breaks something three steps downstream.

### CARD: Signs it is duct tape, not architecture

- You cannot say what you pay for software without opening your bank statement.
- The same customer exists as a different record in three or more places.
- Someone in the business says "I think that's connected."
- You have found out an automation was broken from a customer, not a system.
- Only one person can safely change anything.
- You are paying for at least one tool nobody has opened this quarter.

> Four or more is the point at which adding another tool makes it worse, not better.

## The reframe

The instinct, when the stack misbehaves, is to go looking for the tool that fixes it. A better connector. A smarter CRM. Something with an AI badge on the pricing page.

That instinct is why you have eleven subscriptions.

Software vendors are structurally unable to solve stack sprawl, because their business model is to be one more tool in it. Every one of them is optimising for its own category, and none of them is responsible for what happens in the gap between their product and the next one. That gap is yours. It always was.

So the question changes. It stops being "what tool am I missing" and becomes "what is it costing me to run a stack nobody designed." Those are different questions with different answers, and only one of them has an ending.

## The honest limit

Consolidation is the wrong answer for some businesses, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of thing that makes you stop trusting the rest of this.

If one of your tools is genuinely load-bearing and specialised, a clinical system, a trade-specific job management platform, a piece of compliance software your industry mandates, do not rip it out to tidy up your stack. Keep it, connect what needs connecting, and consolidate around it.

If your problem is that nobody knows you exist, a better-organised stack will not fix that. Systems make existing demand convert. They do not create demand.

## FAQs

### What is a tech stack for a small business?

Your tech stack is every piece of software the business runs on and every connection between those pieces. For a service business that usually means a CRM, an email platform, a booking tool, a website or funnel builder, a chat widget, a phone system, a payments tool, and whatever is wiring them together. The connections are part of the stack even though nobody invoices you for them, and they are where most of the maintenance cost sits.

### How many tools should a small business be running?

There is no correct number, and anyone giving you one is guessing. The better test is how many places the same customer exists, and how many of your tools depend on a connection to another vendor to be useful. One customer record with fewer dependencies beats a lower tool count achieved by dropping something you actually needed.

### Is an all-in-one platform genuinely cheaper than separate tools?

Usually on subscriptions, yes, though it depends entirely on what you are currently running. OS A.I's plans are $97, $297 and $497 a month. For category context, HubSpot's Marketing Hub Professional was listed at $890 a month on annual billing for three seats plus a $3,000 one-time onboarding fee, checked 18 August 2026. The comparison worth making is not the headline price, it is the price plus onboarding plus the tools each option lets you cancel. Do that arithmetic with your own numbers rather than ours.

### Does an all-in-one platform mean being mediocre at everything?

It is a fair objection and the honest answer has a condition attached. For a business with one to ten staff, a connected 8-out-of-10 across every function generally beats five disconnected 10-out-of-10s, because the losses are happening in the gaps rather than inside any one tool. For a larger operation with a specialist requirement and someone whose job is to maintain integrations, the calculation genuinely changes.

### What does OS A.I replace?

Across ten categories: CRM, email marketing, funnels and landing pages, websites, scheduling, automation, live chat, routine phone handling, courses and community, and per-channel inboxes. The full named list is in the table above. What it replaces in your business depends on what you are running, which is why the audit comes before the conversation.

### I have tried consolidating before and never finished. What is different?

Nothing, if you do it the same way. That is the most common objection we hear and it is also the truest one, which is why the setup is the product rather than an add-on. The done-for-you build exists because consolidation fails when the owner has to run the migration themselves in the gaps between client work. If you go the self-serve route, plan the switch-off of each old tool as a dated task with an owner, or you will end up maintaining two stacks.
