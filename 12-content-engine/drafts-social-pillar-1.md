## The argument these posts make, in order

From the cluster, section 2. Worth keeping in view, because each post is one step of it:

1. You already generate enough enquiries to be busier than you are
2. The ones you lost, you lost in the hours before you replied
3. That is structural, not a discipline problem
4. More advertising into a leaky process buys more leaks
5. Follow-up should not depend on a person being available

**What the brand refuses to say:** that OS A.I will get you more leads. It responds to the ones you
already have.

# E1 · Monday · LinkedIn · sourced stat

## W1 — the 23 per cent
**Voice: Tumai.** The best number in the database.

```
In 2011, Harvard Business Review audited 2,241 companies to see how quickly they answered a web enquiry.

Twenty-three per cent never answered at all.

For everyone else, the average first reply took 42 hours.

Both groups paid to generate that enquiry. One group paid twice, because the person went and bought from whoever got back to them first.

The study is fifteen years old and still the one everybody cites. Buyer patience has not improved since.

The difficult part is that you cannot tell from the inside which group you are in. Nobody writes in to say they gave up waiting.

Submit an enquiry through your own website tonight. Time the reply.

#SpeedToLead #LeadResponse #SmallBusiness #CRM
```

## W2 — the 42 hours
**Voice: Tumai.**

```
Forty-two hours is the average time a business takes to answer a web enquiry.

That is from the Harvard Business Review audit of 2,241 companies, published in 2011.

Think about what 42 hours actually covers. An enquiry on Thursday evening gets answered Saturday afternoon. One that lands Friday lunchtime gets answered Sunday night, which in practice means Monday.

Nobody decides to take two days. It is what happens when replying depends on someone noticing.

The separate MIT study on this, by James Oldroyd in 2007, found that answering within five minutes made qualifying a lead 21 times more likely than answering at thirty minutes.

Not 42 hours against five minutes. Five minutes against thirty.

The window is narrower than the calendar makes it look.

Check your last ten enquiries. Note when each one arrived, and when it was answered.

#SpeedToLead #LeadResponse #SmallBusiness #Sales
```

## W3 — the 21 times
**Voice: Tumai.** The misattribution note is deliberate. It is cheap credibility and it is true.

```
Replying to a web enquiry within five minutes makes qualifying that lead 21 times more likely than replying at thirty minutes.

Five minutes against thirty. Not against a day.

That figure is from Dr James Oldroyd's Lead Response Management study, run through MIT and InsideSales in 2007. It gets attributed to Harvard Business Review constantly, including by people selling software. Worth having the right source if you ever quote it.

Twenty-five minutes is the whole difference. That is one phone call you could not step out of.

Which is why this is a structural problem rather than a discipline one. No amount of being organised makes a person available at 7pm on a Saturday, or at 11am while they are on a job with their hands full.

The fix is not replying faster. It is that replying should not depend on a person being free.

Count how many of your enquiries arrive outside the hours you are at a desk.

#SpeedToLead #LeadResponse #AI #SmallBusiness
```

## W4 — the first hour, into the CRM argument
**Voice: Tumai.** Bridges to [cluster-crm](../11-topic-clusters/cluster-crm.md) and to the
`article/crm-discipline-problem` branch.

```
Answering a web enquiry inside the first hour makes meaningful qualification about seven times more likely than answering in the hour after it.

One hour against two. Harvard Business Review, 2011, the same audit of 2,241 companies.

I find that figure more useful than the dramatic ones, because an hour is achievable. Five minutes sounds like it needs a different business. An hour sounds like a process.

The CRM connection is not the one most people expect.

Most small business CRMs fail because nobody uses them. The data goes stale, the reminders get dismissed, and within a few months it is a contact list with eighteen-month-old notes in it.

That is not laziness. A CRM that depends on someone remembering to open it has handed the critical step to the busiest person in the building.

When did you last open yours on a day that went badly?

#CRM #SpeedToLead #SmallBusiness #Systems
```

## W2 — when you don't need a CRM
**Voice: Tumai.** Real disqualification.

```
Some businesses should not buy a CRM, and I would rather say that now than three months into a subscription nobody opens.

If you take fewer than about five enquiries a week, you do not have a data problem. You have a short list, and a notebook will beat software, because the notebook is already open.

If all your work comes from two referral sources and you know both people by name, a CRM adds a step between you and a relationship that already works.

If nobody has written down what happens to an enquiry after it arrives, a CRM will not tell you. It will record an undefined process faithfully.

The honest test is volume and repetition. A CRM earns its place when more comes in than one person can hold in their head, and when the same sequence happens often enough to be worth describing once.

Below that, you are buying structure you do not need yet.

#CRM #SmallBusiness #Systems #Sales
```

## W3 — why the spreadsheet won
**Voice: Tumai.**

```
I have watched businesses move from a spreadsheet to a CRM and go backwards. The reason is worth understanding before you spend anything.

The spreadsheet wins on things people underrate.

It opens instantly and shows everything at once. No record view, no clicking into a contact to find out what happened.

It bends to how you actually work. A column added on a Tuesday because you needed it exists by Tuesday afternoon.

And everybody already knows how to use it.

What a spreadsheet cannot do is act. It will not message anyone at 7pm. It will not notice that a lead has gone quiet for nine days. It sits and waits for a person, which is the constraint you already had.

So the question is not which one is better. It is whether what you need next is a clearer view, or something that does the follow-up without you.

If it is the view, stay on the spreadsheet and keep the money.

#CRM #SmallBusiness #Automation #Systems
```

## W4 — your CRM isn't a discipline problem
**Voice: Tumai.** Pairs with the `article/crm-discipline-problem` branch when it merges.

```
Your CRM is not a discipline problem.

Most owners I talk to carry some guilt about this. The CRM exists, it was set up with good intentions, and the notes in it stop around eight months ago. The explanation offered is always about themselves. They got slack. They got busy. They are not a systems person.

That explanation is wrong, and it is expensive, because it points at the wrong fix. If the problem is you, the answer is to try harder, and trying harder has already been attempted.

A CRM that needs a human to open it, remember what stage someone is at, and decide what to send next is a system with a person as its engine. On a good week the person runs it. On a week with a staff absence and a job that goes sideways, the person is the first thing to stop.

The test is not whether you update it. It is whether anything happens when you do not.

Open yours and find the oldest contact with no next action against it.

#CRM #SmallBusiness #Systems #Automation
```

# E7 · Tuesday · Facebook · diagnostic question

Genuine diagnostic questions, not rhetorical openers. Rhetorical-question openers are banned;
[hooks-and-openers](../09-content-banks/hooks-and-openers.md) permits these because the reader has to
actually answer them. No hashtags on these.

## W1
```
What happens to an enquiry that arrives on a Saturday?

Not in theory. This Saturday just gone.

Someone fills in the form at 10am. Where does it land, and who sees it before Monday?

For most businesses the honest answer is that it sits in an inbox, and the person who would answer it is not working, which is correct, because it is Saturday.

The enquiry does not know that. It is comparing you to whoever did reply.
```

## W2
```
Why are you losing leads you already paid for?

Not the ones that never came in. The ones that did.

Someone clicked, filled in the form, and then nothing happened for long enough that they sorted it out another way.

That lead cost you the same as the ones that converted. It just did not get the same chance.

Go and look at last month's enquiries that went nowhere. Check what time they arrived.
```

## W3
```
How long does a new enquiry wait before a human sees it?

Pick a number before you look. Most people guess about an hour.

Then measure it across your last ten.

The gap between the guess and the measurement is the thing worth fixing. It is usually widest in the evenings and on weekends, which is also when people have time to send enquiries.
```

## W4
```
Open your CRM and look at the oldest contact in it.

What is the next action against that person?

If the answer is nothing, that is not a filing issue. It is a lead somebody paid for, sitting in a list, waiting for a human to remember them.

There is usually a row of them.
```

# E3 · Wednesday · LinkedIn · operator story

**This is the one slot I cannot draft, and the reason matters.**

A story post needs a specific, checkable detail. The only legitimate sources for that are
[origin-story](../01-company/origin-story.md), [how-we-work](../01-company/how-we-work.md), and
discovery calls once they are recorded. Writing a client anecdote from the cluster's framing would be
inventing a fact, which guardrail 2 forbids and which is what makes most B2B social indistinguishable.

Four prompts for Tumai or Ariki. Each needs one real detail: a day, a tool, a number, a sentence
somebody actually said.

| Week | Prompt | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| W1 | The moment you first noticed that the lost deals were lost before anyone replied. What were you looking at | Ariki |
| W2 | A thing you believed about follow-up when you started, and what changed it | Tumai |
| W3 | Walk through what you do in the first week of an onboarding, and name the step clients are most surprised by | Ariki |
| W4 | Something in the ten-step onboarding that exists because an earlier version went wrong | Ariki |

**W3 and W4 double as Pillar 3 groundwork for December**, and they publish process rather than
results, which is the only proof currently available.

**Record these as voice notes and transcribe them.** Written from scratch they will come out flat;
spoken, they arrive in the right register and most of the editing is cutting.
